An Astrobiologist onsidering these images could construct a highly speculative, yet internally consistent, hypothesis for a previously unrecognized lithobiological system on Mars—a putative “rock‑worm” life cycle in which mineralized structures masquerade as ordinary regolith clasts while executing slow, radiation‑powered metabolisms in the near‑surface environment. 
The starting point of the proposed life cycle is the composite unit that resembles a small “mushroom” flanked by triangular or horn‑like elements—analogous to a bat’s head with **mandibles**—perched on or partially emerging from the host rock.    
In an astrobiological reinterpretation, these mandible‑shaped outgrowths could function as mechanically resonant acoustic antennae, optimized to couple with the low‑frequency elastic and electromagnetic fluctuations (ELFs) propagating through the regolith and thin atmosphere during dust storms and thermal tides.      

​

One can imagine each conical element as a piezoelectric composite of silicate grains and iron oxides, where stress from incident acoustic waves induces charge separation, generating weak electrical potentials that the organism senses through embedded semiconductor‑like mineral phases.      The images provided, showing mushroom‑like caps, conical “mandibles,” and nearly perfect spherules embedded in and resting upon fine sediment, are morphologically reminiscent of Martian spherules (“cocoons”) and erosional pedestals, yet here they are re‑interpreted not as purely abiotic concretions, but as potential exoskeletal stages of a lithic organism adapted to this extreme regime.

  Hatching: emergence of rock‑worms
    (“psyhrrongian slugs)

Once sufficient structural and chemical reorganization has occurred, the pod’s outer rind fractures along inherited weakness planes, giving rise to elongate, slug‑like protrusions that wriggle free into the interstitial spaces of the regolith: the rock‑worm stage. 
To a conventional camera these entities would remain near‑indistinguishable from small clasts or mudstone fragments, but under this speculative scenario their internal architecture supports limited locomotion via ratcheting microfractures and grain‑scale rearrangements, akin to a glacier’s creep but accelerated to geologically fast, biological timescales of months or years.        Because many such “mandibles” within a local population appear canted in a similar azimuth, the hypothetical organism might align its sensory lobes along gradients in ELF energy density, much as terrestrial plants orient towards light or magnetotactic bacteria align with geomagnetic fields, but here using the coupled seismic–electromagnetic environment of the Martian boundary layer.

Detachment and aeolian transport: larval clasts

At some threshold of structural fatigue or developmental maturity, the “hooda‑mushroom” fractures along pre‑engineered mineral seams, and the cap plus mandibles detach as discrete clasts—the larval stage—liberated onto the surrounding slope. 
Mars is well known for its vigorous aeolian regime; wind tunnel experiments and in‑situ observations at the Bagnold Dunes demonstrate that even modest gusts can entrain sand grains into saltation, scouring and mobilizing pebbles and coarse fragments in repetitive hops and rolls.

​

​
In this speculative framework, the freed larval pieces are not passive debris but evolutionarily tuned for aeolian reshaping: their initial geometries maximize torque under shear flow, ensuring that they roll and tumble rather than remain static.    Over many storm cycles the abrasion and mutual impacts gradually erase sharp edges, driving the larval clasts toward low‑energy, nearly spherical configurations—what an unsuspecting geologist would catalogue as ordinary concretions or impact‑rounded pebbles, but which in this narrative are the organism’s next ontological stage: encapsulated **pods** or “eggs.”

 Thermal gestation in the regolith

The Martian surface experiences substantial diurnal temperature swings, with the upper few centimeters of regolith cycling through tens of degrees in a single sol, while fine sand exhibits efficient radiative coupling to both sunlight and the thin atmosphere.   
For a mineralized organism, these diurnal thermal gradients provide a natural engine for slow chemical reaction networks: differential expansion, phase transitions of adsorbed volatiles, and redox processes at grain boundaries can all drive charge separation and diffusion on microscopic scales.   
​

​
Inside a rolling pod, the repeated mechanical jostling in dust storms, combined with cyclic heating from sun‑warmed sand, becomes a quasi‑incubational environment.   
Porous internal microstructures inherited from the original rock allow trapped brines, perchlorate residues, or adsorbed atmospheric gases to migrate through a network of catalytic mineral surfaces, gradually assembling and re‑patterning organic or organometallic polymers that serve as the organism’s informational substrate.
